🧪 Acids, Bases and pH: Formulas and Calculators

pH is a logarithmic way to describe acidity. Because it is logarithmic, a change of one pH unit means a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.

Key formulas

What you findFormulaUnit
pHpH = −log₁₀[H⁺]–
pOHpOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]–
Hydrogen ion concentration[H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴmol/L
Hydroxide ion concentration[OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴmol/L
pH and pOH at 25 °CpH + pOH = 14–
Ionic product of water at 25 °CK_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴mol²/L²

pH + pOH = 14 holds at 25 °C only. The calculator takes [H⁺] directly, so for a weak acid you must first find [H⁺] from the acid’s dissociation, not just its concentration.

Worked example

A solution has [H⁺] = 0.001 mol/L. Find pH and pOH. Then find [H⁺] when pH = 4.5.

  1. pH = −log₁₀(0.001) = 3.
  2. pOH = 14 − 3 = 11 (at 25 °C).
  3. For pH 4.5: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁵ ≈ 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L.

Try the same numbers in the calculators above to check each step.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming [H⁺] equals the acid concentration for weak acids. That is true only for strong acids.
  • Forgetting the minus sign in the logarithm.
  • Using pH + pOH = 14 at temperatures other than 25 °C.
  • Using natural log (ln) instead of base-10 log.

Quick questions

What pH is neutral?

pH 7 at 25 °C. At other temperatures the neutral point shifts slightly because K_w changes.

Why can pH be negative or above 14?

Very concentrated strong acids or bases can have [H⁺] above 1 M or [OH⁻] above 1 M, giving pH below 0 or above 14.
